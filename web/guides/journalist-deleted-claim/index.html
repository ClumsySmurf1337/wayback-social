Journalist: tracking a deleted claim

A reporter hears that a public figure deleted a claim. The workflow: broad search, deleted diff, citations, verification — and getting comment before anything is published.

Illustrative example — not a real person

Every name, handle, and post in this case study is invented to show the workflow. No real person is depicted, and no real post is quoted. The scenario: a fictional city councillor, "Councillor Dana Reyes" (@danareyes_city, fictional), is said to have promised a new riverside park in 2021, then deleted the post after the project was cancelled. A reporter wants to know whether the post existed, what it said, and when it vanished.

The workflow

  1. Broad search first. On the console, type the handle and tick All networks with a date range covering 2021. A public figure usually has captures on more than one network; the spread itself is evidence of a real history. Pro covers all 14 networks and 2,500 captures per search — a newsroom job, not a single-post lookup.
  2. Narrow to the claim. The broad search returns every capture. Use the date range to narrow to the months around the promise, then read the capture rows. The post URL and its capture timestamps are the spine of the story — every claim you publish must carry a date.
  3. Run the deleted diff. Research tools → Deleted diff checks each archived post live. The 2021 post answers 404 on the live site: deleted. Neighbouring posts from the same month answer 200: live. The contrast is what makes the deletion visible — a single 404 proves nothing on its own.
  4. Build citations. Research tools → Citations turns the results into a list with the archived URL, Wayback timestamp, snapshot link, live verdict, and retrieval date. That is the citation format your editor and your readers can check.
  5. Verify the snapshot yourself. Open every replay link at web.archive.org. A capture that replays as a blank shell proves the URL was captured on that date, not the content — if the text doesn't replay, say so in your notes and look for the text via oEmbed or a second archive (Arquivo.pt, Ghostarchive) before you quote it.
  6. Get comment before publishing. The archive proves a post existed and was removed. It does not prove why. Ask the councillor — or their office — for comment, and report the answer alongside the capture. Publishing a screenshot of a replay as if it were a live post, without comment, is how an archive search becomes a rumour.

What not to do

  • Don't publish a replay screenshot as a live post. Label every archived image with its capture date.
  • Don't treat a single capture as a story. One capture can be a misdated mirror, a different account with the same handle, or a placeholder. Corroborate: multiple captures, multiple networks, a second archive.
  • Don't skip the "unknown" verdicts. A post marked unknown was login-walled or errored — it is not deleted, and reporting it as deleted is an error your correction will have to fix.
  • Don't use the tool on private individuals the same way. This workflow is for public figures and public claims. Checking a private person's deleted posts for a story needs a public-interest reason and an editor's sign-off — see Trust & safety.

Triage, not court-ready. If the claim ends up in a filing rather than a story, the evidence pack (Studio) preserves a dated, citable record with a SHA-256 manifest — but it carries no vendor affidavit and no chain-of-custody statement. See Evidence pack for lawyers.

Honest limits

Public archived posts only. Found ≠ guilty. Not found ≠ not real.
